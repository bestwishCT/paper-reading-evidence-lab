51: 3 Automatic Evaluation
52: Our benchmark measures the following three di-
53: mensions of system responses:
54: • Fluency: whether the model’s generated text is
55: fluent and coherent.
56: • Correctness: whether the answer is accurate
57: and covers all aspects of interest.
58: • Citation quality: whether the answer is well
59: supported by the cited passages and no irrelevant
60: passages are cited.
61: In the following, we present automatic metrics for
62: each dimension and discuss why the combination
63: of the three metrics provides a robust evaluation.
64: 3.1 Fluency
65: We use MAUVE (Pillutla et al., 2021) to evaluate
66: the fluency of the output (§C). We deploy MAUVE
67: for ASQA and ELI5 and omit it for QAMPARI, as
68: QAMPARI only requires a list of short answers as
69: the response and LLMs consistently adhere to the
70: format in our experiments. As MAUVE is sensitive
71: to output length and text style, and most LLMs are
72: capable of producing fluent text, we mainly employ
73: it as a sanity check as long as the MAUVE scores
74: are high enough.
75: 3.2 Correctness
76: Our objective is to measure the informativeness and
77: utility of the generation to the question. Liu et al.
78: (2023) propose to directly evaluateperceived utility
79: by humans, a process difficult to automate. There-
80: fore, we use correctness—whether the response is
81: accurate compared to a ground truth answer—as a
82: proxy. Evaluating the correctness of long-form gen-
83: eration is a challenging task (Krishna et al., 2021),
84: and we describe our strategy for each dataset be-
85: low. Figure 2 illustrates the metrics and we include
86: additional implementation details in §C.
87: For ASQA, we follow Stelmakh et al. (2022)
88: and calculate the recall of correct short answers by
89: checking whether the short answers (provided by
90: the dataset) are exact substrings of the generation
91: (exact match recall; EM recall).
92: For QAMPARI, we follow Rubin et al. (2022)
93: and calculate the precision and recall of the model
94: prediction, by checking the exact match to the gold
95: answer list. We add one additional adjustment:
96: considering that users often want to know only a
97: few example answers of the question, our evalua-
98: tion considers recall to be 100% if the prediction
99: includes at least 5 correct answers (recall-5).

34: sub-claims. To ensure that the generated sub-
35: claims are of good quality, we manually inspect

38: sub-claim, we assign a score of 1 if it is relevant to
39: the question and faithful to the facts presented in
40: the ground truth, and 0 otherwise. We found that
41: 112 out of the 120 (93.33%) sub-claims received a

43: are of high quality and faithful to the ground truth.
44: Furthermore, the average number of words in the
45: generated sub-claims is 14 words, and they are
46: typically just one sentence long. This is aligned
47: with the intent behind the metric—to capture short
48: factual claims made by the original answer.

50: of sub-claims. We further analyze our sub-claim
51: evaluation metrics by checking the error rate of
52: the final prediction of the NLI model. To this end,
53: we first manually annotate the entailment scores
54: between 40 outputs and their sub-claims (in total
55: of 120 pairs; these are the same questions from the
56: previous analysis). We then use the NLI model
57: to obtain the entailment scores for the output and
58: sub-claims. Using the human annotations as the
59: ground truth label, we found that the NLI model

41: E Citation Recall Discussion
42: Our citation precision evaluation cannot detect a
43: citation that partially supports the statement and
44: hence will falsely penalize it. Consider a state-
45: ments3 and its citations [2][4][5]: if [2] entails
46: partial information ofs3 that [4][5] also entails,
47: [2] will be counted as “irrelevant” while it should
48: not be penalized. Liu et al. (2023) conduct hu-
49: man evaluation on citation precision in a different
50: way: For each citation, they ask annotators to judge
51: whether the citation (1) fully support, (2) partially
52: support, or (3) does not supportsi. One citationci,j
53: is precise if (a)ci,jfully supportssi or (b)Ci fully
54: supportssi,ci,jpartially supportssi, and noc∈Ci
55: alone fully supportssi. This evaluation solved the
56: corner case we mentioned in the main paper (one
57: citation partially supports the claim but is identi-
58: fied as “irrelevant”). However, it is challenging to
59: conduct such evaluation automatically, as there is
60: no existing model that can judge whether a cita-
61: tion “partially” supports a claim. We also explore
62: prompting ChatGPT to conduct such a task, which
63: yields poor results. We defer it to future work to
64: collect supervised data to train a betterϕthat can
65: detect “partial support”.
